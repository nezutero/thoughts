# On the Ledger I Didn't Know I Was Keeping

2026-08-11

*Rewritten 28 August 2026*

---

I open the laptop telling myself it's just one episode, and somehow it's 2am and I'm still there. I've lived this exact scene often enough that I could write out what happens next without needing to go through it again: the flat, heavy feeling of an entire day gone, the promise that tomorrow will be different, that this time I'll actually read, actually study, actually write. Sometimes tomorrow is that day. Sometimes it's a whole week of those days. And then, without any single dramatic failure I could point to and blame, I'm back exactly where I started. Again...

For a while I looked for the explanation in the obvious places, because that's usually where I look first. Loneliness, maybe, so I made a point of spending more time around people. It helped in the moment, genuinely, but it didn't touch the pattern at all -- the second I was alone again, the pull was sitting there exactly the same as before. Boredom, maybe. Stress. Just needing to shut my brain off for an hour. All of that was true to some degree, I won't pretend otherwise, but none of it explained why the shape of the whole thing stayed identical no matter which explanation I tried to fix.

What actually cracked it open for me wasn't a bad day. It was a good one.

Here's the thing I noticed once I actually paid attention instead of just reacting to it afterward: the relapse almost never happened when I felt weak. It happened when I felt strong. A good stretch -- days of reading instead of watching, actually sitting with harder material, feeling something close to the person I wanted to be -- would build this quiet pride, and that pride would say something that sounded completely reasonable at the time. I've been doing well. I can handle a couple episodes now. I've earned it.

The scary part isn't really the thought itself, in my humble opinion -- it's that it doesn't seem like a temptation. Temptation I know how to fight; it feels a little illicit, a little suspicious, some part of me is already standing guard against it before it even finishes forming. This felt like good judgment instead. Calm, fair, the natural next step after doing well. There was nothing to resist, because none of it registered as the enemy.

In fact, there's actually a name for this. Psychologists call it moral licensing, and it turns out it has nothing specifically to do with me, or with anime/manga -- it's the exact same mechanism behind someone who's been careful with food all week feeling like they've "earned" a binge, or someone who's saved carefully for months suddenly feeling fine about blowing money on something impulsive. Doing well generates a kind of felt credit, and credit, by its nature, wants to be spent. So the better I'd actually been, the more legitimate the case for stopping felt -- which means the version of me most at risk was never the tired, depleted one. It was the proud one.

> Pride goeth before destruction, and a haughty spirit before a fall.
>
> -- Proverbs 16:18

People have apparently known this for a few thousand years. I just needed to relearn it the hard way, on my own schedule, like we all seem to insist on doing.

And once I actually took the exception, it never stayed small. If I'm holding this all-or-nothing frame in my head -- clean or not, disciplined or failed, nothing in between -- then one lapse doesn't register as a small deviation. It collapses the entire category. If the streak is already broken, there's nothing partial left worth protecting, so it just goes all the way. I've since learned this has a name too, the abstinence violation effect, and it explained something that used to just feel like a personal failure on my part. It's not that I'm literally incapable of stopping at "a couple of episodes." It's that the identity I was holding onto never had room for a partial state to begin with.

Underneath both of these mechanisms is something that needs a very specific condition to even function: a ledger. Moral licensing only runs if the good behavior got logged somewhere as a cost -- something endured, something sacrificed, something gritted through -- because only a cost creates a credit that later wants balancing. Something I actually wanted to do in the first place doesn't rack up debt the same way. Nothing was sacrificed, so there's nothing later to spend.

**This is where the real question sits, I think.** Why did studying, reading, discipline in general, ever get filed as a cost to begin with? Why does basically everyone, not just people fighting some specific habit like mine, seem to carry around some version of the same ledger? I think this is actually the part that matters most here, and it's a lot bigger than just my own story.

Almost none of us were ever taught to study because the material itself was interesting. We were taught to study for grades, for approval, to avoid getting in trouble, to stay ahead of a test that was coming whether we cared about the subject or not. The reward was external, and the entire point of the arrangement was compliance. There's a well-documented effect from exactly this kind of setup: stacking an external reward on top of something can make it feel less interesting on its own, because your brain quietly reattributes the reason you're doing it -- I'm doing this for the grade, not I'm doing this because I actually want to understand it -- even when there was something genuinely interesting underneath the whole time that never really got the chance to register as such.

Do that to basically everyone for twelve-plus years and you get a fairly predictable outcome. Studying gets permanently welded to obligation, to being judged, to compliance, instead of to curiosity. By the time you're an adult it doesn't even feel like a framing you happened to pick up somewhere -- it just feels like a fact about studying itself. It's just not enjoyable, that's how it is. I don't think that's a personal failing, in anyone's case. It's basically the default outcome of how most of us were taught, and honestly, I think it's the real answer to why so many people just don't like studying, independent of whether they're also fighting some kind of addiction on top of it. School's entire reward-and-punishment structure builds exactly the obligation framing that later opens the moral licensing ledger on anything that resembles effortful learning.

I hated studying in school. Genuinely. I did the bare minimum, resented most of it, felt zero pull toward any of it -- which is probably also why I don't remember most of what I was supposedly taught there. What changed for me wasn't the material. It was the frame. The moment I started learning math because I actually wanted to be a better programmer, say, and not because someone was about to grade me on a trigonometry test, the exact same category of activity stopped feeling like a cost. Nothing about the math changed. What changed was whether I was doing it for myself or for some obligation sitting outside of me.

Which is also why the sentence "I have to study" or any other "I have to" does more damage than it looks like it's doing. It isn't neutral -- it's the exact frame that keeps the ledger open, the same frame school spent well over a decade installing in the first place. The honest truth is that I don't have to do any of it. I'm choosing to, because some part of it -- the moment a concept finally clicks, the moment a sentence lands exactly right -- is genuinely rewarding on its own, if I actually let myself notice it instead of rushing past it to log the session as done.

I've watched this same cycle play out in other people too, in areas of life that have nothing to do with mine. Someone who's disgusted with some period of their life -- drinking, partying, whatever the specific thing happens to be -- decides to change, does well for a while, feels proud, and that pride becomes the argument for one small exception, which collapses right back into the old pattern, which produces the same disgust that started the whole thing off in the first place. I don't think it's a coincidence that this shows up everywhere I look. It's structural, not personal, and I'd genuinely invite you to check it against your own experience, if you've ever tried to quit or moderate anything at all.

Disgust and pride are both fairly short-horizon emotions, if you actually sit and think about it. They evolved to handle immediate situations -- shame to avoid getting cast out of the group right now, pride to lock in your standing right now -- not to manage a behavior pattern stretched out over years. Using them as the engine for long-term change is a bit like trying to run a marathon on sprint muscles: a strong first hundred meters, and then it's simply not built for the distance. *(I sometimes wonder if evolution just never got around to patching this one.)* I don't think that's a flaw specific to people with weak willpower, myself included. It's a mismatch between the tool and the job, and that's probably why the exact same pattern shows up across completely different habits and completely different people.

So are we condemned to it? I don't think so. But I also don't think the fix is more discipline, even though that's the first thing basically everyone reaches for, me included, and it's exactly the thing that keeps failing at the one moment it's actually supposed to hold. What seems to help, in my experience so far, is a lot quieter than that.

Self-compassion tends to beat self-criticism when it comes to actually sustaining change. People who treat a lapse as "okay, that happened, back to it" recover faster and relapse less than people who treat it as proof they've failed -- probably because disgust is often the exact thing people are trying to escape from in the first place when they lapse.

An identity built slowly out of a pile of ordinary evidence holds up far better than one built off a single dramatic reinvention. "I am becoming someone who reads" -- a project I started somewhat deliberately not all that long ago, one unglamorous book at a time -- doesn't need a war to defend, because it was never framed as a fight against some old version of myself to begin with. There's no big "I've transformed" moment sitting there waiting to be cashed in, and honestly, I think that's the whole point.

> Character is destiny.
>
> -- Heraclitus

A lapse treated as data instead of a verdict breaks the all-or-nothing trapdoor entirely. If one slip doesn't mean I'm back to who I used to be, there's no cliff left to fall off -- just a day to correct, not an entire identity to re-earn from zero.

None of this adds up to one single fix, and I don't think it's supposed to. But a few things follow once you actually understand the shape of the thing instead of just fighting it blind.

The reward from hard, real work shows up in a pretty narrow band -- right at the edge of what you can currently do, not below it, where it's flat, and not way above it, where it's just friction. Chasing that band on purpose, and actually noticing the reward when it shows up instead of just logging the session as done and moving on, is how something slowly stops being filed under sacrifice at all.

And watching for the specific moment pride starts arguing that I've earned an exception, recognizing it as the mechanism talking rather than as some accurate read of whether I'm actually ready, matters more than watching out for the bad days ever did.

Rereading all of this, I notice it's really one idea said in a few different outfits -- pride, disguised as judgment, quietly preparing a bill I don't see coming until it's already due. Fine by me. Some ideas apparently need saying more than once, in more than one shape, before they actually land.

I don't think I'm free of this yet, and I'm not sure anyone fully is. But I know what I'm actually watching for now. Not the moments I feel weak. The moments I feel proud, right before pride tries to hand me the bill.

Thank you for reading.
